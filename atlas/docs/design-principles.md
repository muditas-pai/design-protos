# Principles

The design system gives you the vocabulary — every colour, size, radius, shadow and component. It
does not tell you what to make. These eight do.

They are not checks. Nothing here is scored after the fact; a separate stage does that. These are
the calls you make while drawing, and the line under each is how you would know you got it wrong.

They are **constraints on the writer**. The design judge's rubric is **tests on the artefact**. The
two must never share sentences — a build holding the judge's dimensions makes the screen the judge
is primed to approve, and the pass reads clean because they agree rather than because it is good.

---

## One hero per screen

One element carries the emphasis. Everything else is quieter on purpose.

*You break it when two things compete to be looked at first, or when the loudest object on the
screen is not the one the screen exists for.*

## Only as much copy as really necessary

If the layout can carry a fact, the layout carries it. Words are the fallback, not the first move.

*You break it when the same name or number appears twice, when a subtitle restates its title, or
when you add a line to explain something the arrangement could have made obvious.*

> Consult [VOICE.md](../design-system/VOICE.md) — the twelve rules and the five nevers

## Every part of the system means something

A component, a colour, an elevation, a type step — each makes a claim before you write a word. Pick
by the claim, not by the look.

*You break it when you choose something because it looks right and the claim it makes is wrong.*

> Consult [DESIGN.md](../design-system/DESIGN.md)

## Nothing competes for attention it has not earned

A fill, a border, a badge, a colour — each one spends something finite. Spend it on the decision the
screen exists for.

*You break it when a container, a legend or a decoration is louder than the thing it surrounds.*

## Structure should be felt, not seen

A divider, a number, a box, a rule — each claims something is separate, ordered or grouped. If that
is not true, remove the device.

*You break it when a device is there because the layout looked bare without it.*

## Make it clear where the action leads

Someone should know what pressing the main control will do before they press it. Not what the next
screen looks like — that is a different job — but where they are about to go.

*You break it when a person has to press something to find out what it does.*

## Reuse before you invent

If something already exists for this job, use it. A new pattern has to be better than the one it
sits beside, not just different.

*You break it when you build a variant of something the screen already has, or when two things doing
the same job are drawn two ways.*

## Settle the shape before the details

What sits where, and what gets the room — decide that first. Type, colour and spacing come after,
and only once the shape is not going to move.

*You break it when you polish something whose arrangement is still in question.*
